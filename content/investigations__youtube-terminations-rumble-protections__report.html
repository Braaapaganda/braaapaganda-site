YouTube Channel Terminations, Braaapaganda, and Whether Rumble Actually Protects Creators Better

Executive summary

This investigation finds a strong, well-documented procedural story but an unresolved substantive one. The supplied records establish that YouTube permanently terminated the Braaapaganda channel on October 16, 2026 for what it characterized as “severe or repeated” violations involving harmful or dangerous content and the promotion or facilitation of certain illegal or regulated goods; that its initial notice and appeal denial did not identify an offending video, URL, product, timestamp, or policy subclause; and that, four days later, both a YouTube support specialist and a manager expressly refused requests for even one example. The records also contain YouTube representations that human reviewers considered the appeal and a secondary review. They therefore do not support saying the appeal was decided solely by a bot. [S01]

The evidence does not establish whether Braaapaganda actually violated YouTube policy. Historical research-peptide advertising and affiliate links are a material possible explanation, and a September 25 creator email indicates that ad reads had stopped while use of a bio link had only recently begun. But the exact published placements, destination pages on October 16, products promoted, applicable drug or pharmaceutical status, and YouTube's actual allegation remain unknown. “Peptide” is not synonymous with “illegal drug,” and a supplier's research-only language would not constitute YouTube approval. [S01]

YouTube had broad published authority to terminate accounts for material or repeated breaches, and its Community Guidelines expressly contemplate immediate termination for severe abuse or a channel dedicated to violating policy. A clean strike history therefore would not, by itself, entitle a creator to a warning first. At the same time, YouTube's U.S. Terms say it will notify a user of the reason for suspension or termination, subject to exceptions including legal requirements, investigation integrity, security, and risk of harm. Its strike system ordinarily gives considerably more particularized information, including what content was removed and what policy it violated. [S02][S03]

The central accountability question is thus narrower and stronger than “YouTube banned an innocent motocross creator”: Can an appeal be meaningfully contestable when the platform identifies a policy category but refuses to identify the act, item, or sanction rationale? YouTube has legitimate anti-evasion interests, but it is difficult to see why those interests necessarily require withholding a content identifier, the applicable subclause, a short description of the prohibited conduct, and whether permanent termination rested on “severe,” “repeated,” “dedicated,” or some other basis. Those particulars can generally be separated from classifier signals, reporter identities, thresholds, and backend detection logic. The Santa Clara Principles recommend that kind of item- and rule-specific notice and meaningful appeal, although they are voluntary civil-society standards, not U.S. law. [S04]

Rumble does not presently offer demonstrably stronger enforceable creator protections across the board. Its current binding platform terms, last modified September 1, 2026, reserve very broad discretion to remove content without notice and terminate access with or without notice. Rumble's much more creator-protective 2022 proposal—a graduated strike system, 15-day appeal to a creator panel, and public decisions—was expressly a proposal. A later SEC filing said Rumble still lacked a formalized removal-and-appeals process at that time and was not obligated to implement the proposed changes. This investigation did not locate a 2026 binding rule, operative creator-panel procedure, or public decision archive proving that proposal is now in force. That absence is not proof that Rumble has no informal appeal practice. [S05][S06][S07]

Rumble does have some concrete portability advantages while an account remains accessible: its support documentation explains how creators can download original uploaded video files, and its licensing choices include a nonexclusive Rumble Player option. But those are not equivalent to a post-termination export guarantee. Rumble's separate Locals service has its own terms and a post-termination provision for retrieval of defined “Member Data”; that should not be conflated with either Rumble.com or a guaranteed video archive. Rumble Studio, meanwhile, is principally a streaming-production tool, not a separately verified creator due-process regime. [S08][S09][S10][S11]

Bottom line: The defensible podcast finding is not that YouTube necessarily got Braaapaganda wrong, nor that Rumble is categorically safer. It is that YouTube imposed its most severe sanction while, in the supplied case record, refusing to disclose enough incident-specific information for an outsider—or arguably the creator—to test the merits or proportionality of that sanction. Rumble's marketing and smaller scale do not establish a lower wrongful-removal rate, and its binding terms provide less explicit procedural protection in several important respects.

The most probative passage is the October 20 exchange. The creator explicitly distinguished a request for his own offending content from a request for enforcement methodology. Azi nevertheless said internal policy prevented sharing specifics to keep partners from “going around” policy. The affiliate evidence substantially changes how the episode should be framed. An August supplier email contemplated research-oriented peptide promotion and no human-use claims. A September 25 creator email said ad reads had stopped but that the bio link had only just begun being used. Those facts make an outbound promotional link a serious investigative lead. They still do not establish what products were behind that link on October 16, whether any were prescription-only pharmaceuticals or controlled substances, whether the destination changed, whether old descriptions contained links, or whether YouTube relied on any of it. [S01]

That matters because YouTube's current regulated-goods policy reaches more than the audiovisual subject matter of a video. It applies to descriptions, comments, livestreams and external links, and it prohibits facilitating access to listed regulated goods; the current page specifically warns that links through which users can purchase hard drugs, poisons or pharmaceuticals without a prescription can lead to channel termination. [S12] YouTube's external-links policy separately says affiliate content is not inherently prohibited, while links directing users to content that violates Community Guidelines can themselves violate policy. [S13] The correct factual inquiry is therefore not “were peptides mentioned?” but what was being promoted, how, through which placement and destination, under what policy text on October 16?

Corrections to the prior dossier and TinyFish work

The earlier AI-assisted research should not be treated as corroboration. The new research supports the following corrections:

  • A France-specific January 2026 contract is not a proper governing source for this U.S. case. The U.S. YouTube Terms retrieved here are the version effective December 15, 2023. [S02]
  • The 4h45m between E01 and E02 is not the duration of appeal review because the appeal submission time is missing. [S01]
  • The October 20 support chat does not establish that the “secondary review” happened during the chat. Shai did not answer the creator's direct question about its date. [S01]
  • “No prior strikes” does not create a binary choice between severe wrongdoing and bot error. Immediate termination is expressly possible for severe abuse, and other factual possibilities remain open. [S03][S14]
  • Current help text referring to two appeals cannot be converted into a claim that Braaapaganda was legally or operationally entitled to two substantive merits reviews. YouTube's November 2025 FAQ described one reviewed appeal; the current help page uses different language. The date, scope and operational meaning of that change are not established here. [S14][S15]
  • The November 13, 2025 statement about better descriptions and timestamps was an announcement of work in progress, not a completed universal guarantee. [S15]
  • Rumble's website terms, Creator Program rules, Locals terms, Studio tooling, and upload-license choices are different instruments. They cannot be collapsed into a single “Rumble creator contract.” [S05][S10][S11][S16]
  • Failure to locate an operative Rumble community-appeal interface is evidence of an unverified implementation claim, not proof that no appeal practice exists.

YouTube's operative framework: authority, notice, appeals and the reason-giving problem

Contractual authority

YouTube's U.S. Terms reserve the right to suspend or terminate an account or access where there is a material or repeated breach, a legal requirement, or a reasonable belief that conduct creates or could create liability or harm. The Terms separately say YouTube will notify the user of the reason for termination or suspension unless giving notice would violate law, compromise an investigation, compromise the integrity, operation or security of the service, or cause harm. [S02]

That language gives YouTube substantial contractual discretion. It does not, however, answer how much specificity counts as a “reason.” YouTube could argue that “severe or repeated harmful-and-dangerous violations involving promotion or facilitation of illegal or regulated goods” is a reason within the contract. The counterargument is that such a category does not tell the user which act supposedly breached the rule and therefore cannot be meaningfully rebutted or cured.

The contract also undermines any claim that termination necessarily means all stored copies were physically destroyed: the Terms contemplate circumstances in which YouTube may retain server copies after removal or deletion. [S02] Separately, YouTube's current termination help says a terminated creator cannot download their YouTube content, although other Google data may still be downloadable. [S14] Those are different propositions: loss of creator access is documented; destruction of every stored copy is not.

Strike and escalation rules

YouTube's ordinary Community Guidelines framework generally begins with a warning. A first strike carries a one-week posting restriction; a second within 90 days carries a two-week restriction; and three strikes in 90 days may result in channel removal. But YouTube expressly reserves termination without warning for severe abuse, and its termination guidance also identifies channels dedicated to policy violations as a basis for termination. [S03][S14]

The current regulated-goods and harmful/dangerous policies likewise describe a graduated enforcement architecture while retaining exceptions for severe or repeated abuse and dedicated violative channels. [S12][S17] The creator's reported no-strike history is therefore relevant to proportionality but not dispositive of policy compliance.

There is also an important scope distinction. YouTube's strike help page says the strike email will tell the creator what content was removed, which policy it violated and what happens next. [S03] That is stronger specificity than appears in E01/E02. But the strike page should not be quoted as though it creates an identical contractual requirement for every whole-channel termination.

Appeal commitments

YouTube's current public explanation of its moderation system says it uses automated systems and human review, that sufficiently high-confidence cases can be decided automatically, and that many flagged cases go to trained reviewers. It says that when a content decision is appealed, a human reviews the appeal case by case. [S18] That public commitment is consistent with Azi's statement that authorized human reviewers handle appeals and is one reason the podcast should not assert that Braaapaganda's appeal was bot-only.

The present termination page says creators have up to one year to appeal, states that creators “can only submit two appeals within the 1 year period,” and also refers to limits on appealing an individual termination. It warns that already-reviewed or excessive submissions can receive automated responses. [S14] By contrast, YouTube's November 13, 2025 moderation FAQ said its team reviews one appeal per channel and described repeated appeal submissions as receiving automated responses. [S15]

That discrepancy is reportable; the safe formulation is: official guidance changed or diverged, but the effective date and scope of the change have not been verified. It would be misleading to say Braaapaganda definitely had an unused second substantive appeal.

The unresolved historical-policy problem

The U.S. Terms are version-dated and therefore can be tied to the incident more confidently. The Help Center rules for regulated goods, external links, strikes and termination are dynamic pages. This investigation retrieved current official pages and historical official material, including the November 2025 FAQ, but was not able to authenticate exact October 16, 2026 archived snapshots of every relevant Help Center page through the available archive access.

That limitation matters. The report can say what the official pages said when checked on October 28 and what dated earlier material said; it should not claim, without an authenticated snapshot, that every sentence of the current regulated-goods or appeal page was identical twelve days earlier.

The tension over reasons

YouTube's November 2025 FAQ acknowledged creator demand for greater specificity and said the company was working on more specific policy descriptions and timestamps. It did not promise that those improvements were complete or that every termination would contain a timestamp. [S15]

The strongest criticism is therefore not “YouTube violated a guaranteed timestamp rule.” It is that three layers of its public framework point toward understandable notice—contractual notice of a reason, strike communications identifying removed content, and an announced effort toward greater specificity—while the supplied case record shows support personnel expressly declining all item-specific particulars, including even a title or link. [S02][S03][S15] [S01]

Substantive merits, proportionality and the anti-evasion defense

Three questions should remain analytically separate.

Authority: YouTube plainly reserves substantial power to terminate channels, including immediately in sufficiently serious cases. [S02][S14]

Merits: The public record is insufficient to determine whether Braaapaganda's actual content or links violated the relevant rule. The factual predicates needed for that determination include the exact item, exact external destination at the relevant time, products or services being promoted, the creator's actual wording, whether multiple placements existed, whether they remained accessible after September 25, and the operative policy language.

Proportionality: Even if some content violated policy, a different question is whether permanent termination of the entire channel—and the associated bar on using or possessing other YouTube channels—was reasonably matched to the conduct. YouTube's circumvention rule makes termination a particularly consequential sanction because it can extend beyond the deleted channel. [S14]

A workable proportionality framework is:

Decision tree — accessible text equivalent

  1. Identify the exact item, conduct, destination and policy version.
  2. Is a violation established?
    • No: Restore the channel and associated access; correct related restrictions; consider revenue/data remediation.
    • Cannot tell: Provide minimum particulars; preserve evidence; conduct genuinely reviewable reconsideration.
    • Yes: Establish the sanction predicate below.
  3. What sanction predicate is established?
    • Isolated or remediable: Remove the link/video; warning or strike; temporary feature restriction; conditional reinstatement.
    • Repeated but curable: Temporary suspension; required cleanup; monitored reinstatement.
    • Severe abuse, dedicated violation, or deliberate circumvention: Would a narrower remedy inadequately control the harm?
      • No: Temporary suspension; required cleanup; monitored reinstatement.
      • Yes: Permanent termination becomes more proportionate, provided reasons are specific enough to contest and meaningful review exists.

This is an analytical decision tree, not a statement of U.S. law or a claim that YouTube is contractually required to offer each lesser remedy.

What must be established before “severe or repeated” can be independently assessed

For “repeated,” the necessary factual record would ordinarily include more than the existence of a channel: the number of violative items, their timing, their similarity, whether earlier material remained live, and whether prior enforcement or notice occurred. A formal prior strike is not necessarily required by YouTube's policies, but repetition itself requires some identifiable plurality of conduct.

For “severe,” the relevant facts would include the nature of the item or destination, the degree of prohibited facilitation, foreseeable harm, whether the content directly enabled a transaction, and why a lesser remedy would not address that harm. YouTube does not have to disclose proprietary thresholds for an independent observer to ask what conduct made the case severe.

For “dedicated to a policy violation,” the overall purpose or substantial operation of the channel becomes relevant. A motocross commentary channel with an allegedly violative sponsor placement presents a different factual picture from an account primarily created to facilitate prohibited sales. YouTube's public rules recognize dedicated-violation termination as a separate category. [S14]

Could YouTube disclose more without exposing enforcement systems?

Yes, at least in principle. The Santa Clara Principles recommend individualized notices that identify the content or account action, the rule violated and enough information to support an appeal; their appeal standard calls for a meaningful opportunity to present information and, where appropriate, human review independent of the original decision. [S04]

A disclosure could safely say, for example:

“A link in the channel profile, present on [date], directed users to a page offering [category of regulated product]. We determined that this violated subsection [X]. Permanent termination was selected because we classified the conduct as [repeated/severe/dedicated], based on [high-level reason].”

That would not require disclosure of model scores, keyword lists, matching rules, risk thresholds, internal investigative queries, reporter identities, evasion signatures or backend architecture.

YouTube's anti-evasion argument remains substantial. Abuse actors can test enforcement boundaries, and identifying every detector signal could make systems easier to game. YouTube's U.S. Terms expressly recognize security and investigation-integrity exceptions to reason-giving. [S02] But the case presents a narrower issue: whether the existence of those legitimate secrets justifies withholding even the alleged act. On the present record, YouTube has not publicly explained that distinction.

U.S. law: what not to claim

Calling an ordinary YouTube termination a First Amendment violation would be legally unsound absent a state-action theory. In Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck, the Supreme Court reiterated that the First Amendment constrains governmental actors, not private entities merely because they provide a forum for speech. [S19]

Likewise, Moody v. NetChoice dealt with constitutional challenges to state laws regulating platform moderation and recognized the relevance of platforms' editorial choices; it does not decide whether YouTube fulfilled a private contractual notice promise in this case. [S20] Section 230(c)(2) also provides federal protection for certain good-faith restrictions of material a provider considers objectionable, but it is not a simple answer to every possible contract claim and does not itself establish the adequacy of notice here. [S21]

The podcast can therefore make a strong fairness and accountability argument without overstating constitutional law.

Rumble: binding protections versus proposed protections

Rumble's current Website Terms are far more important than its branding. The terms reviewed were marked last modified September 1, 2026. They give Rumble broad content discretion, including the right to prohibit, refuse, delete, move or edit content for any reason, at any time, without notice. The termination provisions likewise reserve discretion to terminate access with or without notice and encompass upload, comment and revenue privileges. [S05][S22]

Those clauses do not prove harsh day-to-day moderation. They do mean that a creator cannot point to the present general terms as a strong contractual guarantee of prior warning, particularized notice or independent appeal.

The 2022 “Rumble Rules”

Rumble's June 2022 announcement proposed unusually concrete safeguards: limited automated flagging, manual review for other categories, a graduated removal-notice process, a 15-day appeal period, review by a creator panel of at least nine members, short decision deadlines and publication of outcomes. It also preserved immediate termination for egregious or clearly unlawful cases. [S06]

But “proposed” is operative. Rumble subsequently told investors in a 2023 SEC filing that it did not then have a formalized content-removal and appeals process, described the proposal as intended to formalize existing informal practices, moved the anticipated implementation date and expressly said it was not obligated to implement the changes. [S07][S23]

As of the October 28, 2026 research cutoff, this investigation did not verify an operative binding version of the proposed creator-panel system, a current public appeal entry point implementing those provisions, or the promised archive of appeal decisions. That is an implementation gap in the public evidence, not affirmative proof that Rumble never handles appeals.

Creator Program, Studio and Locals are separate

Rumble's Creator Program has additional terms. The 2026 Creator Program rules allow Rumble to modify, postpone or cancel the program and remove participants where it believes program requirements have been violated. Those rules concern participation and monetization and should not be presented as the platform-wide account-termination appeal regime. [S16]

Rumble Studio is a production and multistreaming tool. Current support documentation explains connections among Rumble, YouTube, Locals and other RTMP destinations; this research did not identify a separate Studio moderation contract conferring stronger termination-review rights. [S11]

Locals is a distinct subscription/community product with its own terms. Those terms permit termination at the company's discretion and say it has no general obligation to back up user content, but they contain a provision allowing a creator to retrieve defined “Member Data” for 90 days after termination. That “Member Data” provision should not be advertised as a guarantee that every creator video file will remain downloadable. [S10]

Portability and licensing

Rumble.com does offer a useful operational safeguard: its help center provides a procedure for downloading original video files from an accessible account. [S08] But the reviewed materials did not establish a post-termination download guarantee.

Its licensing choices matter substantially. Rumble's own support documentation distinguishes exclusive video-management licensing, video management excluding YouTube, the nonexclusive Rumble Player license, and Personal Use. The Rumble Player option allows the creator to manage the same content elsewhere; exclusive management options can be much more restrictive. [S09] Current platform terms should also be reviewed for any additional licenses granted to Rumble, including the current machine-learning language, before a creator treats “uploading to Rumble” as a single standardized rights arrangement. [S24]

Scale, automation and comparable evidence

The latest official quarterly enforcement dataset located by the October 28 cutoff was YouTube's April–June 2026 Community Guidelines Transparency Report. It reports 2,732,395 channels removed worldwide. Dividing by 91 days gives an average of about 30,026 channels per day. That is a calculated quarterly average—not an observed daily series, not a U.S.-only figure, not a count of people, and not a count of entire Google accounts. [S25]

The same dataset attributes 2,310,181 channel removals to spam, deceptive practices and scams, or approximately 84.55%. It separately reports 86,686,063 videos removed because their channels were terminated. Those videos should not be mixed with the separate count of individually enforced videos. [S25]

For individually removed videos, the report lists 11,159,676 removals, of which 11,005,420 were first detected through automated flagging—approximately 98.62%. That figure is frequently vulnerable to misreporting. It is not the percentage of final video decisions made automatically, and still less the percentage of channel terminations made automatically. [S27]

No trustworthy official global channel false-positive rate was established. Nor was an official percentage of channel terminations decided automatically. YouTube's transparency methodology describes several ways channel actions can arise—including accumulated strikes, a single severe violation and accounts dedicated to violation—but those categories do not yield a channel-level automation-error rate. [S28]

The prior dossier's secondary appeal figures should therefore remain out of headline graphics unless they can be traced to the primary report with a clear denominator. Even a verified appeal-reversal percentage would describe a self-selected population of appellants, not the false-positive rate among all terminations.

Documented evidence that mistakes occur

YouTube publicly acknowledged a multi-channel error on October 3–4, 2024 involving channels incorrectly removed for Spam & Deceptive Practices and said the affected channels were being reinstated. [S26] That incident proves that erroneous channel removals can occur at scale. It does not establish their frequency, the technical cause of Braaapaganda's termination or a 2026 error rate.

YouTube's own November 2025 FAQ said it had manually reviewed hundreds of social-media complaints outside normal workflows, found that the “vast majority” of terminations remained correct and reversed a small number of nuanced cases. Because those were publicly raised cases chosen through an extraordinary review process rather than a random sample, the exercise is evidence about company review activity—not a representative precision audit. [S15]

Academic evidence about notice and appeals

A 2024 New Media & Society study analyzed 250 English-language creator videos concerned with YouTube governance. It found complaints about corporate communication in 29.6% of its coded sample and complaints about appeals in 10.8%; the authors specifically described creator frustration over YouTube's failure to specify which portion of a video violated policy. [S29][S30]

That study is context, not a wrongful-removal denominator. Its dataset was deliberately assembled from videos in which creators publicly discussed platform governance; the authors themselves identify language, sampling and community-representation limitations. [S31] It is useful to say “Braaapaganda's complaint resembles a pattern documented in qualitative creator research.” It would be unsound to say “X percent of YouTube bans are unexplained” on the basis of that paper.

No independently verified major motocross channel-termination comparison case meeting a strong evidentiary standard was established in this research. That absence should remain an absence rather than being filled with weak forum anecdotes or unrelated celebrity cases.

Reporting package for the podcast

Strongest platform counterarguments

YouTube's best substantive answer is that its regulated-goods rules can apply to sponsor links and metadata even when the main editorial content is motocross commentary; that a channel need not already have three strikes when the conduct qualifies as severe abuse; that fraud, prohibited sales and other adversarial behaviors are routinely optimized around disclosed enforcement rules; and that the company says authorized humans reviewed this appeal. Those points are all compatible with the current public framework. [S03][S12][S18]

Its hardest unanswered question remains: why would identifying one offending item, the relevant policy subclause and the high-level sanction basis reveal an enforcement technique? The supplied transcript shows that the creator explicitly asked for those particulars while disclaiming any request for detection methods. [S01]

Rumble's strongest answer is different: a company's terms often reserve more discretion than it exercises in routine practice; its public commitment to freer expression may influence real moderation culture; and failure to find a formal public appeal interface does not mean internal support cannot correct errors. Those are plausible counterarguments. The evidence problem is that none establishes an enforceable procedural right or a lower false-positive rate.

Podcast narrative and defensible lines

A rigorous episode should make the unknown allegation the dramatic center rather than automation speculation.

A defensible opening is:

On October 16, YouTube permanently removed Braaapaganda. It alleged severe or repeated violations involving harmful or dangerous content and regulated goods. What it didn't identify was the video, link, product or act behind that allegation.

There is evidence that complicates the story: the channel had historically carried promotion for a research-peptide supplier, and a September email indicates a bio link had recently been used. We do not know whether that is what YouTube acted on.

What we can document is what happened when the creator asked for one example. A specialist and then a manager refused to provide a title, link or timestamp, citing policy integrity and security. YouTube says human reviewers upheld the decision.

So this investigation is not asking you to assume the creator was innocent or guilty. It asks a more basic question: how can a permanent ban be meaningfully appealed when the act justifying it is not identified?

Lines that are supportable:

“YouTube documented the policy category but not the offending item.” [S01]

“The creator's own records disclose a plausible sponsor-link issue, but YouTube has not confirmed that was the cause.” [S01]

“YouTube says appeals receive human review; this record does not prove an automation-only appeal.” [S18]

“No-strike history does not guarantee a warning because YouTube reserves immediate termination for severe abuse.” [S03]

“98.62% is the automated-first-detection share of individually removed videos in Q2 2026, not the automated share of channel bans.” [S27]

“Rumble proposed a notably stronger appeal model in 2022, but this investigation did not verify that those protections became binding or operational by October 2026.” [S06][S07]

“Rumble may be a useful backup distribution platform; that is not the same as proving it has a lower wrongful-removal rate.”

Lines that should be corrected or excluded:

“A bot misunderstood motorcycle slang.” No evidence establishes the trigger or a bot-only decision.

“YouTube gave him no appeal.” It did; the dispute is over specificity and meaningful contestability.

“The appeal was rejected in 4 hours 45 minutes.” Submission time is unknown.

“No strikes means YouTube violated its own policy.” Severe-abuse termination can bypass ordinary escalation.

“All peptides are illegal drugs.” Unsupported and inaccurate as a categorical statement.

“Rumble can't ban you for speech.” Its binding terms reserve broad content and access discretion. [S05]

“Rumble has no appeals process.” Too strong. The defensible finding is that the 2022 formal proposal's implementation was not verified and no corresponding current binding guarantee was located.

The resulting editorial conclusion is deliberately narrower than the earlier dossier's more dramatic possibilities: Braaapaganda is a strong case study in the consequences of permanent platform punishment without item-specific notice. It is not, on the available evidence, proof of an automated motocross false positive, proof that YouTube lacked substantive grounds, or proof that Rumble provides a more reliable adjudicatory system.
