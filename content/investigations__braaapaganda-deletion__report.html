BRAAAPAGANDA · SHOW RESEARCH · OCTOBER 28, 2026

Banned without particulars

United States · YouTube channel termination · Rumble comparison

The strongest supported story is a failure of explanation. The supplied records show that YouTube permanently removed Braaapaganda, upheld the decision, and explicitly refused requests for an offending video, link or timestamp—even after a manager joined the conversation. They do not establish that an algorithm misunderstood motocross, that no human reviewed the case, or that every historical promotion complied with policy. Whether permanent termination was justified remains unproven in the available record. Rumble has not been shown to provide stronger enforceable safeguards or a lower wrongful-removal rate.

Public research edition · October 28, 2026. This report draws on creator-supplied correspondence and public primary sources. Private exhibits are retained outside this site. TinyFish Deep Research supplied an initial report; direct checks corrected material errors. ChatGPT’s dedicated Deep Research tool was not run. No right-of-reply questions have been sent to either platform.

The refusal: the central scene

On October 20, Rob asked for one offending video, link, product or other item, and an explanation of the violation. He expressly distinguished that request from a demand for detection methods. At 8:21:03 AM Pacific, the specialist attributed nondisclosure to preventing partners from going around policy. A manager then joined. At 8:57:46, the support manager said:

“As previously discussed, our internal guidelines strictly prohibit us from sharing specific video titles, links, or timestamps to ensure policy integrity.”

The next message invoked backend security and declined an individualized breakdown. Rob's question about when the secondary review occurred also went unanswered. The transcript documents an hour-long support conversation, not an hour-long policy review. E07, pp1–3 (private record).

Reason-giving tension: YouTube's US terms dated December 15, 2023 promise reasons for termination, subject to investigation, security, legal and harm exceptions. They allow termination for material or repeated breaches and certain risks of harm or liability. The terms do not expressly guarantee a video timestamp for every termination. A blanket policy category may satisfy YouTube's interpretation of “reason”; whether it satisfies the contract in this case is not resolved here. Ask which exception, if any, was invoked and why identifying one item would trigger it. US terms: Notice for Termination or Suspension.

A promise worth testing: in its November 13, 2025 official FAQ, TeamYouTube said it was working on more specific policy descriptions and timestamps. That is a documented improvement effort, not a guaranteed rollout date or a universal right to timestamps. Ask whether it now covers channel terminations and how this refusal fits. Official FAQ, closing paragraph.

The strike help page separately promises identification of removed content. That is a useful comparison, but its scope is strikes; it cannot simply be transplanted into a universal termination guarantee. Strike basics.

Ethical assessment: An appeal is weakened when the creator must guess the allegation. Confidential detectors, reporter identities and abuse thresholds can deserve protection. That does not by itself explain withholding every title, URL, disputed act and applicable subclause. A credible safeguard would provide the minimum particulars needed to contest the decision, with narrowly explained redactions where necessary. This is a proposed standard of fairness, not a finding that all nondisclosure is unlawful.

The voluntary Santa Clara Principles provide a useful benchmark: individualized notice, identifiable content, the relevant rule, meaningful opportunity to respond and review with appropriate independence. They are civil-society recommendations, not a US statute or an adjudication of this case. Santa Clara Principles: Notice and Appeal.

Was permanent termination justified?

Separate three questions. Authority: do published rules allow immediate termination in some circumstances? Yes. Merits: did the actual material meet those circumstances? Unknown. Proportionality: why was permanent channel exclusion necessary instead of a narrower response? The supplied notices and support exchange do not answer that question with case-specific facts.

YouTube's ordinary strike process generally starts with a warning, but explicitly allows termination without warning for severe abuse. A reported clean strike history is therefore relevant mitigation, not an absolute defense. Copyright blocks are a separate process and do not establish this Community Guidelines violation. Strike basics.

The regulated-goods rule prohibits facilitating access to specified goods and warns that certain pharmaceutical or drug purchase links can lead to termination. It covers descriptions and verbal directions as well as videos. A motocross channel can therefore encounter that rule through a sponsor placement without its race commentary being prohibited. But “peptide” alone does not identify the applicable category, product status, intended use or prohibited conduct. Regulated-goods policy.

The external-links policy says affiliate content is not inherently prohibited; the destination and conduct matter. It also describes narrower link or video removals in some circumstances. That supplies a concrete question about alternatives, not a guarantee that a creator may cure every violation after detection. External-links policy.

The new original-email evidence matters. On August 9, the supplier proposed disclosed, research-focused advertising and no human-use claims. On September 25, Rob wrote that ad reads had stopped and the link had just been placed in his bio. This supports neither an all-links-removed claim nor a proven October 16 violation. Supplier assurances are not platform approval. Personal purchase history and draft scripts are not substitutes for the published material. E08 (private record).

How the system works—and what remains hidden

YouTube describes a combination of machine detection and human review. High-confidence systems can make automatic decisions; other flags go to trained reviewers. Its published description says humans review appeals. This explains the public workflow, not which path Braaapaganda followed or the classifier's internal logic. How YouTube reviews content.

The current termination help page allows up to a year to appeal, mentions two appeals within that period and a per-termination limit, and warns that previously reviewed or excessive submissions can receive automated replies. Its November 13, 2025 FAQ instead described one substantive appeal per channel. The change date and interaction between these limits were not established. Do not promise another review or interpret every automatic acknowledgment as proof the original review was automated. Current termination guidance; 2025 FAQ.

The termination guidance also says creators lose the ability to download YouTube content. That is a serious practical consequence, but it does not prove destruction of every server copy. Download data for channel terminations.

Policy-version limit: the public pages were checked October 28. The US terms display a 2023 date and Rumble's terms a September 1, 2026 modification date. An authenticated October 16 snapshot of every relevant YouTube help rule was not obtained. Do not imply that current help text conclusively proves the exact incident-date wording.

The numbers the show can responsibly use

The primary transparency report retrieved covers April–June 2026: 2,732,395 channels removed worldwide. Dividing by 91 days yields approximately 30,026 channels per day, a quarterly average. It is not a US count, a unique-person count or a count of entire Google accounts. Spam/deceptive-practices/scams accounts for 2,310,181 removals, about 84.55%. Google transparency report, Q2 2026.

The same report lists 11,005,420 of 11,159,676 individually removed videos as first detected automatically: about 98.62%. This is video first detection, not automated final decisions or channel termination share. A defensible global channel-error rate and daily wrongful-removal estimate were not established. Appeal overturn rates, even if available, would be a selected sample with different denominators. Hypothetical error scenarios would not be actual estimates.

No independently verified set of major motocross termination cases was established. The episode should not imply a demonstrated sport-wide purge. No sport-wide pattern should be inferred from this case alone.

Does Rumble offer better protection?

Its terms, last modified September 1, 2026, permit discretionary content removal without notice and access termination for any reason, with or without notice; revenue collection can be affected. A zero-tolerance provision also leaves suspension versus termination to Rumble. Those clauses do not establish how often the powers are used, but they undermine a promise of guaranteed immunity from unexplained exclusion. Rumble terms: content discretion and Termination.

In June 2022, Rumble proposed limited automated flagging, manual review, escalating notices, a 15-day appeal to a creator panel and public decisions. Immediate termination remained possible for egregious cases. The announcement anticipated implementation later in 2022; its status as a proposal must remain visible when quoting it. Rumble's June 16, 2022 proposal.

A 2023 SEC prospectus subsequently said no formalized removal-and-appeals process existed then, shifted expected implementation to the end of 2023, and stated there was no obligation to implement changes. This is historical confirmation of delay—not proof the system is still absent in 2026. No operative community-panel interface, public decision archive or current implementation confirmation was established in this research. 2023 prospectus: content-moderation risk and Terms of Service discussion.

Decision for the show: Rumble may be useful as another distribution outlet, but this research cannot certify better procedural rights or a lower error rate. Even a more permissive editorial environment would answer a different question from reliable notice, appeal and restoration. Keep recordings, audience contact and feed/domain control independently recoverable. A backup distribution channel helps continuity; it does not turn discretionary terms into a protection.

The case for unfairness, with the strongest rebuttal

Defensible thesis: Permanent punishment without an actionable explanation fails basic fairness because it obstructs both correction of a genuine mistake and rebuttal of a false accusation. The more severe and durable the penalty, the stronger the duty to explain why it is necessary. This is the show's ethical judgment, grounded in the documented refusal; it does not require pretending the affiliate evidence does not exist.

YouTube's strongest response: large-scale abuse requires fast intervention; particular examples can expose detection weaknesses; commercial promotion may be prohibited even within legitimate journalism; grave or repeated violations can warrant immediate action; reviewers upheld this decision. The fair follow-up is specific: what minimal facts can be supplied safely, what made the violation severe or repeated, and why would removing the identified placement fail to address the harm?

Rumble's strongest response: broad legal reservations may exceed everyday practice, and an informal support process may provide meaningful relief. That possibility deserves examination. Ask for current procedures and outcomes rather than treating either favorable branding or unfavorable contract language as a measured error rate.

Opening script

On October 16, YouTube removed Braaapaganda. The email alleged severe or repeated violations and pointed to illegal or regulated goods. It did not identify a video, a link or a product.

I appealed. Later, I asked support for one specific example. I explained that I was asking about my own content, not how their detection systems work. A specialist refused. A manager refused too, saying their internal guidelines prohibited sharing video titles, links and timestamps.

There is a part of this story I need to put on the table. I had a research-peptide affiliate arrangement. I had stopped the ad reads, and my September 25 email says I had begun putting the link in my bio. That history needs scrutiny. It does not tell us what YouTube actually found.

YouTube says humans reviewed the appeal. I cannot prove a bot made the final decision. What I can document is the refusal to tell me which act justified permanently removing the channel.

Today we are testing three things: whether the rules explain this punishment, whether an appeal can be meaningful without specifics, and whether moving to Rumble really gives a creator better protection.
